Partner Finding

Before a mid-market company buys from SAP, they need the right implementation partner. The finder was supposed to help. It wasn't.

Role

End-to-end UX

Timeline

2025 — ongoing

Team

Pod lead · UX researcher · 3-domain stakeholders

Status

Detail page handed off · Go-live Q3 2026

The Problem

Four ways to lose the same customer.

2

Separate partner finders

3

Detail page types

3

Teams owning different pieces

3,500+

Partners across 120 countries

Partner discovery lived on two domains with two separate finders, three detail page types, and two disconnected data repositories — owned by three different teams, accumulated over years of separate solutions. And the users navigating it are digitally self-served: mid-market buyers new to SAP, evaluating it against competitors, not working through a sales rep. Every friction point is a potential exit to a competitor.

That fragmentation produced four distinct failures. The first — users couldn't reliably find a partner finder at all — was solved structurally: the project consolidated the domains, finders, and data into one experience. That was its founding decision, not a design solution. The remaining three are the problems my design work owns.

02

Matching the right partner

Information wasn't structured for comparison, and the four partner types — Sell, Build, Service, Consult & Implement — overlapped so much that users couldn't tell them apart. Users dropped rather than persisted.

03

Trusting what they read

Between marketing pages, informative pages, and PDF outputs, there was no consistent trust-building experience. Users who didn't feel confident never reached out.

04

Knowing what comes next

After contacting a partner, users had no expectation-setting, no tracking, no continuity — and SAP had no visibility into whether the connection led anywhere.

The trust failure was easiest to see up close. Deloitte had two separate pages on SAP's platform — one marketing-focused (Deloitte presenting themselves), one informative (SAP-structured data). Two pages, same partner, neither explaining the other. For a user who doesn't know the SAP ecosystem, that's confusing at best and suspicious at worst.

Research

One finding changed everything.

Foundational studies, led by our UX researcher, mapped cleanly onto the three design problems. On matching: users don't speak SAP's internal language and don't know what SAP offers — jargon created confusion before the search even started. On trust: too many signals with no way to tell them apart, and on the results page, comparing partners was the single hardest step. On what comes next: the contact form was too generic to tailor to real needs, and whether users would tolerate signing in at all was an open question.

"Partner credentials existed. They just weren't building trust. They were labeled, not explained — and users couldn't tell the difference between what SAP verified and what partners claimed themselves."

I ran a copy test to diagnose whether the confusing partner types were a UX problem or a writing problem. It was writing. The definitions weren't wrong — they weren't explained. I connected the content marketing and copy teams to resolve it in parallel with design, which freed us to focus on the structural redesign.

Key Decisions

Three choices that shaped the detail page.

The Decision

One detail page has to serve both a partner presenting themselves and a customer evaluating that partner. Which user leads?

Options Considered

Partner-controlled marketing page · SAP-structured informative page · Unified page weighted toward informative

Informative over marketing

Credentials, project delivery numbers, and customer stories lead. Marketing language follows. The same page serves both users without compromising either.

The Decision

The same partner is a fundamentally different entity depending on where you are. How do you reflect that on a single page?

Options Considered

Global view only · Separate regional pages · One page with location detection and user override

Global + regional, layered by context

Deloitte Germany has different SAP recognition than Deloitte Singapore. Both matter. The page pre-selects the user's region, keeps them in control, and surfaces both layers — without feeling overwhelming. This required new component pattern combinations within the existing Design System.

The Decision

Users need to compare partners. A comparison feature is significant scope. What enables comparison without one?

Options Considered

Dedicated comparison feature · AI-assisted comparison via Joule (ideal state) · Consistent IA across every touchpoint

Consistent IA for MVP, AI comparison for v2

Same fields, same hierarchy, every partner tile and detail page. Users can compare mentally even without dedicated UI. The long-term vision: AI-assisted comparison through Joule — "which of these is better for a manufacturing company my size?"

Solution

Personalization with a principled fallback.

Even the default state is grounded in evidence.

Ideal state: personalized from the moment a user lands — pre-filtered by industry, company size, and SAP product usage for logged-in users. For logged-out users, behavioral signals inform what surfaces.

When neither is available, partners surface based on verified project delivery numbers — real-world performance data, not self-reporting or marketing priority. The default isn't random. That distinction matters.

Design Detail

The same partner. Two completely different realities.

Global recognition and local reality, on one page.

Deloitte's overall partner level and global competencies sit alongside their regional expertise, local awards, and recognition specific to the user's geography. Same partner, different trust signals depending on where you are.

Location detection pre-selects the region. The user stays in control. This required building new component pattern combinations that hadn't been used in the Design System before — not new components, but new layering logic for holding global and regional data on a single page without overwhelming it.

Solution

The contact form as a value exchange.

The fourth failure — the black box after contact — is being addressed last, and deliberately. Getting users to sign in before contacting a partner enables personalization and makes the connection more qualified. But sign-in has to feel worth it. The design, now in early wireframes, centers on value props that justify the ask, sets expectations for what happens after outreach, and captures just enough information to make the partner introduction meaningful rather than generic.

Open Questions

What's still unresolved — on purpose.

Enterprise design at this scale means shipping with open questions visible, not pretending they're settled. Three are active right now.

Default state

When neither profile nor behavioral data exists, results fall back to verified delivery numbers. But should the default view organize by location or by service? Partner contracts are service-centric; user mental models often aren't.

Ahead of the market

No competitor personalizes partner discovery dynamically. Being first is either an advantage or an unvalidated risk — the next round of testing is designed to find out which.

Sign-on friction

Requiring sign-in before contact could cause drop-off before users ever see the value. When in the journey to ask — and which value props make it worth it — is still being tested.

Outcome

Design is ahead of schedule.

Detail page — MVP and ideal state — handed off to development. Search results page moving to hi-fi. Contact form in early wireframes. Go-live Q3 2026.

The work is structured in phases: consulting with development on what's achievable for MVP, with another round of testing planned before the layered improvements toward the ideal state — proactive partner surfacing on product pages, an SAP-verified indicator on delivery numbers, and AI-assisted comparison through Joule.

What I'd do differently: start the content governance conversation earlier. Partner-managed content with no SAP quality control is a structural problem design can work around but not solve. Earlier cross-functional alignment on content standards would have given us more influence over what the detail page could reliably show.
